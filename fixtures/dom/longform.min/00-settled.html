Floating comes down to density, mass packed into a given volume. Anything less dense than the liquid it sits in will float, and anything denser will sink. Ice floats because it is roughly nine percent less dense than the liquid water beneath it, so the real question is not why ice floats but why freezing makes water less dense in the first place.

As water freezes, the molecules stop tumbling and lock into a fixed hexagonal lattice, each one hydrogen-bonded to four neighbors at a specific angle. That angle forces open, six-sided channels through the structure, like scaffolding built from hexagons instead of a tight stack of bricks. The rigid geometry that makes ice strong is the same geometry that makes it spacious.

The hexagonal lattice is also why snowflakes are six-sided.

It is tempting to think ice floats simply because it is cold, as if temperature itself controls floating. But cold water sinks, not floats, right up until it actually turns to ice. What matters is not temperature directly but the structural change at the freezing point, when molecules snap into that open lattice.

Above 4°C, water behaves as expected: warmer means more motion, more spreading out, lower density. But between 4°C and 0°C, water starts pre-forming small, short-lived patches of that open ice-like structure while still liquid, which lowers density slightly instead of raising it. The result is a density peak at 4°C, with cooler water floating above it and ice floating above all of it.
